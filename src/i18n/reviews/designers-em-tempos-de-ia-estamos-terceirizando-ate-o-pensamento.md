To inaugurate this blog, I decided to talk about a subject that has been keeping many people in our profession awake at night: **The future of design in the age of artificial intelligence.**&#x42;ut before we talk about AI, it's worth remembering that our problems didn't start with ChatGPT.

## Even before AI, things weren't easy.

I'm from Fortaleza, in Ceará, and I've always had the impression that the profession of designer has never received the recognition it deserves here. When I started in a quick-print shop, it was common to find people who thought our work was easy; after all, we just sat in front of a computer. As if all it took was opening Photoshop, pressing a few buttons, and that's it: the artwork was done.

Then came the social media boom. Suddenly, in addition to being a designer, you had to be an influencer, produce content, sell courses, and build an audience. Within agencies, the demand only increased, while the space to explore ideas decreased. **The creative department has turned into a veritable pastry shop of carousels, posts, and stories.** It's not that this work doesn't require creativity, but when everything becomes urgent and focused on quantity, there's little time left to think. I know professionals who gave up on the field during that period. And, honestly, little has improved since then.

## And then came artificial intelligence.

As if the scenario wasn't complicated enough, along came generative AI. First, the concern reached writers and journalists. Soon after, image generation placed designers and art directors at the center of the discussion. At first, the technology seemed like just another support tool: generate an image, process the result, and incorporate it into the composition. But the change was rapid. **Today, we are already seeing entire pieces being generated by AI, with the designer only stepping in to make adjustments and finalize files.** In some companies, what was once a possibility has become a requirement.

The problem isn't using artificial intelligence. I myself recognize its potential. The issue is when productivity starts replacing the creative process and, worse, professional judgment itself.

## Are we outsourcing even our thinking?

I've seen managers and creative professionals asking AI to evaluate visual identities, logos, and graphic pieces. The tool points out possible improvements, and these recommendations reach the designer as changes to be made, often without any questioning. A second opinion is valid, but **When a machine's answer becomes absolute truth, we are outsourcing not only the execution but also the critical thinking.**

A visual identity doesn't appear out of thin air. There's research, briefing, strategy, repertoire, and a series of decisions behind each project. AI can analyze an image, but that doesn't mean it understands all the choices that led to that result. And when we have to justify our work in the face of an automated evaluation, without anyone questioning its criteria, something is amiss.

## What's left for the designer?

Alexandre Wollner, one of the pioneers of Brazilian design, argued that design is a project, not just illustration or decoration. It's curious to think about that now, when we can generate dozens of visual alternatives in seconds. **If design is a project, it cannot be reduced to the final image.**

I don't believe we should fight against AI. The technology is here to stay, and we need to learn how to work with it. But perhaps the greatest challenge of our profession isn't mastering the next tool or writing better prompts. Perhaps it's something more important: **not to give up our ability to think, question, and create intentionally.**

Because, ultimately, if we start outsourcing our critical thinking as well, **What exactly will remain of our work?**
