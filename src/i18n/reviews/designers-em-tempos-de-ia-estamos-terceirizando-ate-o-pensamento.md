For the first post on this blog, I wanted to talk about something that has been keeping many of us in design awake at night: **the future of design in the age of artificial intelligence**. But before we talk about AI, let's remember that our problems didn't start with ChatGPT.

## Even before AI, things weren't easy

I'm from Fortaleza, in Ceará, and I've always had the impression that design has never received the recognition it deserves here. When I started in a print shop, it was common to find people who thought our work was easy; after all, we just sat in front of a computer. As if all it took was opening Photoshop, pressing a few buttons, and that's it: the artwork was done.

Then came the social media boom. Suddenly, in addition to being a designer, you had to be an influencer, produce content, sell courses, and build an audience. Within agencies, the demand only increased, while the space to explore ideas decreased. **Creative departments became production lines for carousels, posts, and stories.** It's not that this work doesn't require creativity, but when everything becomes urgent and focused on quantity, there's little time left to think. I know professionals who gave up on the field during that period. And, honestly, little has improved since then.

## And then came artificial intelligence

As if things weren't complicated enough, along came generative AI. First, the concern reached writers and journalists. Soon after, image generation placed designers and art directors at the center of the discussion. At first, the technology seemed like just another support tool: generate an image, refine it, and work it into a layout. But the change was rapid. **Today, we already see entire designs generated by AI, with designers stepping in only to make adjustments and prepare the final files.** In some companies, what was once a possibility has become a requirement.

The problem isn't using artificial intelligence. I myself recognize its potential. The issue is when productivity starts replacing the creative process and, worse, professional judgment itself.

## Are we outsourcing even our thinking?

I've seen managers and creative professionals asking AI to evaluate visual identities, logos, and graphic work. The tool points out possible improvements, and these recommendations reach the designer as changes to be made, often without any questioning. A second opinion is valid, but **when a machine's answer becomes absolute truth, we are outsourcing not only the execution but also our critical judgment.**

A visual identity doesn't appear out of thin air. Behind each project are research, a brief, strategy, a body of references, and a series of decisions. AI can analyze an image, but that doesn't mean it understands all the choices that led to that result. And when we have to justify our work in the face of an automated evaluation, without anyone questioning its criteria, something is amiss.

## What's left for the designer?

Alexandre Wollner, one of the pioneers of Brazilian design, argued that design is a process of planning and problem-solving, rather than just illustration or decoration. It's curious to think about that now, when we can generate dozens of visual alternatives in seconds. **If design involves planning and problem-solving, it cannot be reduced to the final image.**

I don't believe we should fight against AI. The technology is here to stay, and we need to learn how to work with it. But perhaps the greatest challenge of our profession isn't mastering the next tool or writing better prompts. Perhaps it's something more important: **holding on to our ability to think, question, and create with intention.**

Because, ultimately, if we start outsourcing our critical thinking as well, **what exactly will be left of our work?**
